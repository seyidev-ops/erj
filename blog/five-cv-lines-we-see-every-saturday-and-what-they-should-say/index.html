Five CV Lines We See Every Saturday, and What They Should Say

At the Launchpad, the same five lines turn up on CV after CV. Each one is easy to fix. Here they are, with better versions you can adapt tonight.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 12 December 2026 3 min read

Every Saturday we read CVs live at the Launchpad. Different people, different fields, and yet the same lines keep appearing. None of them is a disaster on its own. Together they make a CV that could belong to anyone, which is exactly the problem.

1. "Results-driven professional with excellent communication skills."

Why it fails: everyone writes it, so it tells the reader nothing, and it takes up the most valuable space on the page.

Try instead: "Operations coordinator with six years in logistics. I keep suppliers, drivers and customers moving on time, and cut late deliveries by a third in my current role. Looking for remote operations roles with teams in European hours."

What, how well, and where you want to go next. Three sentences.

2. "Responsible for managing social media accounts."

Why it fails: it says what you were in charge of, not what happened.

Try instead: "Ran three social media accounts for a retail brand; grew followers from 4,000 to 19,000 in a year and brought in about a fifth of online orders."

3. "Proficient in Microsoft Office."

Why it fails: it is expected, and it hides what you can actually do.

Try instead: name the tools in the adverts you want and what you do with them. "Excel: monthly sales reports with pivot tables and lookups for a team of 12. Google Workspace and Slack daily."

4. "Handled customer complaints."

Why it fails: no scale, no outcome.

Try instead: "Resolved about 30 escalated complaints a week, most within one working day, and wrote the reply templates the team still uses."

5. "References available on request."

Why it fails: the employer already assumes this. It takes a line you could use for evidence.

Try instead: delete it. Add one more result in its place.

The pattern behind all five

Each weak line describes a duty or a quality. Each better line gives a number, a result or a named tool. If you are not sure what number to use, ask yourself three questions about each role: how many, over what period, and what was it like before you started?

You will not always have an exact figure. A fair estimate, honestly described ("about", "roughly"), is better than nothing. Never invent one. An interviewer will ask.
