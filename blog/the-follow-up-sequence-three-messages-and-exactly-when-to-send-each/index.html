The Follow-Up Sequence: Three Messages, and Exactly When to Send Each

Most applications die in silence, and most candidates respond to that silence by sending nothing, or by sending the same anxious "just checking in" four times. There is a sequence that works better.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 19 August 2026 5 min read

You applied. Nothing happened. This is the ordinary case, not the unlucky one.

What you do in the fortnight after an application separates the people who get replies from the people who wait. The difference is not persistence. It is that each message carries something new.

The rule that governs all three

Never send a message whose only content is that you sent a message. "Just following up" adds nothing to the other person's day and reads as pressure without value.

Every follow-up should contain one new thing: a reason, a piece of work, or a decision.

Message one — day three, to a person, not a portal

Find the hiring manager or a recruiter at the company on LinkedIn. Not HR generally; the person who will actually work with whoever is hired.

Short note: you applied on Monday for the specific role, one sentence on the single most relevant thing you have done, and a line naming why this company rather than a hundred others.

That last part is what makes it survivable. "I applied for the support role and would love to be considered" is noise. "I applied Monday for the Customer Support role — I spent three years handling billing escalations for a fintech with about 20,000 users, which looked a lot like what your help centre describes" is a person.

Message two — day ten, with something attached

If there has been no reply, send one more. This time it carries work.

A short document, a two-minute recorded walkthrough, a link to something relevant you built. Not a portfolio dump — one artefact, tied to their problem.

For a support role: five example replies to the kinds of complaints their product would generate. For a data role: one chart answering one question about their public numbers. For a writing role: a rewrite of a page on their own site, with two sentences on what you changed and why.

This is a very small amount of work and it converts at a rate that surprises people the first time they try it. You have stopped being an applicant and become someone who has already started.

Message three — day twenty, and it closes the loop

The last one is short and gives them an easy exit.

"I'll assume the role has moved on — no reply needed. If anything similar opens later I'd be glad to hear about it. Good luck with the hire."

This one is not for them. It is for you: it ends the open loop so you stop refreshing an inbox over a role that closed three weeks ago.

It also, reliably and often, produces a reply. People answer a message that asks nothing of them far more readily than one that asks for a decision.

Then stop

Three messages over twenty days. After that, the silence is the answer, and the only useful response is more applications, not more follow-ups.

Nobody has ever been hired by a fourth chase. Several have been quietly blocked by one.
