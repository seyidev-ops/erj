EMEA, Worldwide and “Remote”: Reading the Location Line Honestly

The location line is the most important sentence in a remote advert and the one most people skim. What each common phrase usually means, and the two-minute check that saves a wasted application.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 10 October 2026 6 min read

An application to a role you cannot legally be hired for is not a long shot. It is a certain no that takes three weeks to arrive.

The location line decides that before anyone reads your CV, so it is worth reading the way an employer’s payroll team would.

The common phrases

“Remote”, and nothing else. Tells you where the work happens, not who can be hired. Many of these are quietly limited to one country. Treat it as unknown until the advert or the company says otherwise.

“Worldwide” or “Anywhere”. The strongest signal you will see, provided the description does not contradict it further down with a time zone, a tax status or a work-rights line.

“EMEA”. Europe, the Middle East and Africa on a map. On a payroll it frequently means “the European countries where we have an entity”. If Africa or Nigeria is not named, check before you invest an evening.

“Remote — Africa” or a named African country list. Good news, and worth checking the list: some include Nigeria, some include only the countries where the company already employs people.

The two-minute check

  1. Read the location line and the last third of the description, where the restrictions usually hide.
  2. Search the advert for “authorised”, “eligible”, “time zone” and “resident”.
  3. Open the company’s careers page and see where its other remote roles say they hire.
  4. If it is still unclear, it goes in the “ask first” column, not the “apply tonight” one.

Why this is a supply problem, not a confidence problem

People who skip this check usually conclude they are not good enough, because silence feels personal. Very often the application never had a route to a human at all.
