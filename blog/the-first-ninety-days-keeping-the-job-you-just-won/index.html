The First Ninety Days: Keeping the Job You Just Won

Getting hired remotely and staying hired remotely are different skills. What the first three months are actually measured on, and the three habits that decide them.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 6 October 2026 7 min read

Most remote-work advice stops at the offer, as though the hard part were over. For a first global remote role it often is not, because the thing that gets you hired — evidence of past work — is not the thing that keeps you employed.

What keeps you employed is whether people can rely on you without watching you.

What is actually being measured

Not hours. Not how quickly you reply. Three things:

Does work arrive when you said it would? In an office, a delay is visible and absorbed in conversation. Remotely, a missed date with no message is the entire signal, and it is read as unreliability rather than as a busy week.

Can people tell what is happening without asking? Your manager is managing several people across time zones. The person whose status is always clear is cheaper to manage than the person who does better work but has to be chased.

Do you surface problems early? A blocker raised on Tuesday is a small adjustment. The same blocker discovered on Friday is somebody’s weekend.

The three habits

A visible end-of-day note. Three lines wherever your team lives: what moved, what is blocked, what is next. It takes four minutes and it removes most of the anxiety a distant manager feels about a new hire in another country.

Dates you actually commit to. When asked when something will be ready, give a date you can defend and then tell people before it slips, not after. One early warning buys more trust than three delivered surprises.

Written questions that can be answered asynchronously. “Quick question?” at 11pm their time helps nobody. “I am blocked on X. I have tried A and B. I think the answer is C — can you confirm, or tell me who owns it?” can be answered in one line whenever they wake up.

The first week, concretely

Find out who decides what, what good looks like at day thirty, and where work is actually discussed — every team has an official channel and a real one.

Then produce something small and complete in the first fortnight. Not the big project. A finished, useful, visible thing. It converts you from a hiring decision that people are still quietly evaluating into a colleague who does work.

The mistake that ends first roles

Going quiet when struggling. It is the most human response and the most expensive one, because silence from a new remote hire is indistinguishable from absence.

If you are behind, say so early and propose a plan. Almost every manager can work with that. Almost none can work with discovering it in week six.
