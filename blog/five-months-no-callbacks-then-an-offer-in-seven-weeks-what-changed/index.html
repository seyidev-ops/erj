Five Months, No Callbacks, Then an Offer in Seven Weeks: What Changed

A composite of designers who made the same turn. The work was never the problem — the evidence was. Here is what was fixed, in order.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 25 July 2026 6 min read

Composite again, drawn from several participants with the same shape of problem. The numbers are typical rather than exceptional.

Where he started

Three years designing for a Lagos agency. Genuinely good work. Applying to remote design roles for five months, roughly forty applications, zero callbacks.

His conclusion was that international companies do not hire African designers. That was not what was happening.

What was actually wrong

His portfolio was a gallery. Twelve pretty images with no explanation. A hiring manager looking at it learned that he could make things look nice, which they assumed anyway, and nothing about whether he could think.

His CV listed clients, not outcomes. "Designed marketing materials for X, Y and Z." No indication of what any of it achieved.

He was applying only to large, famous companies. The ones with two thousand applicants per role.

His portfolio was a PDF he emailed. Which meant it often did not arrive, and could not be shared internally.

Week one: three case studies, not twelve images

He cut the portfolio from twelve pieces to three. For each one he wrote four short sections: the problem, the constraint, what he did, and what happened afterwards.

The third one was the strongest, and it was for the client with the ugliest work — because that project had a real constraint and a real result. The prettiest piece did not make the cut.

Week two: rebuilt as a link

Moved everything to a simple site with its own address. Now it could be pasted into an application field, shared in a Slack channel, and opened on a phone.

The link went at the top of his CV under his name, and into the Featured section of LinkedIn.

Week three: changed the target

He stopped applying to companies everyone has heard of and started targeting companies of twenty to eighty people that had raised money in the last year — meaning they were hiring, and had fewer applicants per role.

Fifteen careful applications instead of forty rushed ones, and for each he found the design lead or founder and sent a short message.

Weeks four to six: the actual grind

Two weeks of near silence. Then three replies in four days, which is how it usually goes — nothing, nothing, nothing, then several at once.

Two interviews. He failed the first. Asked to walk through a project, he described what he made rather than why. Afterwards he wrote out the answer he should have given.

Week seven

Second interview, same question, prepared answer. Offer: product design role at a fifty-person European company, $2,100 a month, contract through an employer-of-record.

The lesson, stated plainly

He did not get better at design in seven weeks. He got better at evidencing it. The forty earlier applications failed because they gave a stranger no way to evaluate him.

Most people in month five of a fruitless search are in exactly this position: the ability is there, the evidence is not, and no amount of additional applications will fix an evidence problem.
