Async Work Explained for Someone Who Has Never Worked Remotely

"Async" gets used constantly and rarely defined. Here is what it actually means day to day, and the four habits that make you good at it.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 22 July 2026 5 min read

Async is short for asynchronous, which means people are not working at the same time. That is the whole idea. Everything else follows from it.

In an office, you turn round and ask. In a distributed team, the person you would have asked is asleep. So the work has to be structured so it keeps moving whether or not anyone is available.

What it looks like on an ordinary day

You open your laptop at 9am. There are twelve Slack messages from a team that finished their day while you were asleep, and a document with comments on it.

You read everything first, without replying. Then you answer in a batch — properly, in full, not a trickle of one-liners. Then you work on your own tasks for several hours without interruption, because there is nobody to interrupt you. In the afternoon your American colleagues come online and you overlap for a few hours of live conversation. Before you close, you write your end-of-day note.

Notice what is missing: the constant small interruptions of an office. That is what people mean when they say remote work is more productive. It is not that you work harder; it is that the day has fewer holes punched in it.

The four habits

1. Write things down where others can find them

A decision that lives only in your head or a private chat does not exist to the team. Put it in the channel, the document, or the task card. The rule of thumb: if three people would benefit from knowing, it goes somewhere public.

2. Over-communicate status, under-communicate noise

People should always know what you are working on and whether it is on track. They do not need running commentary. The end-of-day note handles most of this in four lines.

3. Assume good intent in short messages

Written text with no tone reads colder than it was meant. "Can you redo this?" is almost never anger; it is someone typing quickly between meetings. Reading hostility into brevity causes more remote-team friction than any actual conflict.

4. Protect your focus deliberately

Nobody can see that you are concentrating. Set your status, close the tabs, and batch your message-checking into two or three windows a day rather than continuously.

The mindset shift that takes longest

In an office, being visible is part of being valued. Remotely, nobody sees you at your desk and it does not matter. What is visible is your output and your writing.

People who struggle with remote work are usually people who were rewarded for presence. People who thrive are the ones who get comfortable being judged on what they produce and how clearly they explain it.

That adjustment is real and takes a few months. It is worth knowing it is coming.
