Surviving a Remote Probation When Nobody Can See You Working

Three to six months where the company decides whether to keep you. What is actually being assessed, and the four habits that decide it.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 21 June 2026 5 min read

Probation exists everywhere, but remotely it works differently, because the usual signals are missing. Nobody sees you arrive early, stay late, or look busy. So the assessment runs on a narrower set of evidence — and knowing which evidence changes what you do.

What is actually being assessed

Not effort. Not hours. Four things:

  1. Do things finish? Started is invisible. Finished is not.
  2. Do I always know where you are? A manager who has to ask is already annoyed.
  3. Do problems arrive early or late? Early is normal. Late is a trust event.
  4. Does managing you cost more than it saves? This is the real question underneath the other three.

The four habits

The end-of-day note

Four lines before you close the laptop: what finished, what is moving and when it lands, what is blocked and who can unblock it, what you need and by when.

This single habit answers questions two and three permanently. Your manager wakes up already knowing where things stand and never has to ask, which is the fastest route to being trusted with larger work.

Finish something small in week two

Do not wait for something impressive. Update a stale help article, tidy a messy board, document a process nobody wrote down. One completed small thing early builds more trust than a large thing half-done in week eight.

Renegotiate dates before they pass

Slipping is normal. Slipping silently is not. "This will land Thursday rather than Tuesday — the data came in late. Nothing else is affected." Sent on Monday, that is professionalism. Discovered on Thursday, it is a problem.

Surface problems while they are small

The instinct is to hide a difficulty until you have solved it. Remotely that reads as three days of nothing happening. Say it the same day, with what you have tried.

The thirty-day question

Message your manager at the one-month mark:

"I am at thirty days. What is going well, and what should I be doing differently? I would rather know now than at the review."

Almost nobody asks. It is a short, uncomfortable message that gives you two months to fix anything before it counts — and it signals seriousness in a way that no amount of working late can.

What actually ends probations

In order: going quiet when stuck; missing dates without warning; needing to be assigned every task; being unreachable during agreed hours. Notice that none of them are about ability. People are almost never let go remotely for not being able to do the work.

If it is genuinely going badly

Say so before they do. "I do not think I am where I should be at this point. Here is what I think the gap is, and here is what I plan to do about it. Am I reading it right?"

That conversation sometimes saves the job. It always preserves the reference.
