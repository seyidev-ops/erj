Three AI Tasks Remote Employers Now Expect You to Handle

AI fluency in a job advert rarely means building models. It means three ordinary tasks done faster and checked properly, and the checking is the part being assessed.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 16 September 2026 6 min read

AI fluency now appears in adverts for roles that have nothing to do with machine learning, and it makes people anxious in a way the requirement does not deserve.

What the employer means, almost always, is three ordinary tasks. And what they are assessing is not whether you can produce output with a tool, because everyone can, but whether you can tell when the output is wrong.

Task one: draft and compress

Long input, short usable output. A forty-minute call turned into eight decision lines. A twelve-page document turned into a summary a busy person can act on. Rough notes turned into a clear message.

The discipline: never ship the first output. Draft, attack, rewrite. You draft, you ask the tool to argue against what it just produced, you rewrite what survives. What you ship is the third version, and it is yours.

Task two: structure unstructured mess

Fifty support emails into five themes with counts. A spreadsheet of inconsistent entries into consistent categories. Requirements pulled out of a rambling brief.

The discipline: always spot-check ten rows by hand against the source. Categorisation by a language model is confident whether or not it is correct, and the failure mode is not obvious errors but plausible ones. Ten rows takes four minutes and is the difference between a useful analyst and a liability with a progress bar.

Task three: explain and translate

A technical thing in plain language for a customer. A local process explained to someone who has never seen your market. A policy turned into instructions.

The discipline: you must know enough to catch a wrong explanation. Never use a tool to explain something you could not roughly explain yourself, because you will not notice the sentence that is subtly false.

The four-operation test

If an advert names a specific tool, you do not need mastery. You need four operations: create something, change something, share it with the right permissions, and find something you made last month. Four operations per tool is the whole test at interview stage, and it is a two-hour afternoon per tool.

The tools tell on you, though. Do not list a tool you cannot demonstrate those four operations in, because a task stage will find out.

The sentence that answers the question

When an interviewer asks how you use AI, the wrong answer is a list of products. The right answer is a method plus a limit. I use it to draft and to structure, I check the output against the source before it goes anywhere, and I do not use it for anything I could not verify myself.

That says you are fast and you are safe. Employers worry about the second part, and almost nobody addresses it.

Using it on your own application

Yes, use it. As an interrogator, not a writer. Paste the advert and your CV and ask what a hiring manager would doubt. Ask which of your bullets is a claim with no evidence. Ask it to argue against your covering note.

Do not let it write the file. The tell on a generated CV is that it could belong to anybody, and that is precisely what the reader is scanning for.
