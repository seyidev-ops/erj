The Application Ledger: How to Make a Demoralising Process Diagnosable

If you cannot say what happened to your last twenty applications, you cannot tell whether you are being rejected or never being read. One sheet fixes that.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 14 August 2026 6 min read

Ask most job seekers how the search is going and the answer is a feeling. Badly. Nothing is working. It has been months.

Ask what happened to the last twenty applications specifically — how many replied, how many were rejected outright, how many went silent, how many were roles they were actually eligible for — and almost nobody can say.

That is not carelessness. It is that nothing in job hunting produces a record unless you make one. And without a record, the process is not just painful, it is undiagnosable.

Silence and rejection are different diagnoses

This is the whole reason the ledger matters. A stream of rejections means you are being read and judged and found not quite right — a Representation or Aim problem, and a workable one. Total silence usually means you were never read at all: the file did not parse, or you were never eligible in the first place.

Those two situations feel identical from the inside. They require opposite responses. Without a record you cannot tell which one you are in, so you guess, and most people guess wrong in the same direction: they rewrite the CV again when the problem was that half those companies could never have hired them.

Eight columns, nothing clever

A spreadsheet is enough. Date applied. Company. Role. Where you found it. Company type — can they hire and pay someone in your country, yes, no, or unknown. Tailored, yes or no. Outcome — silent, rejected, screen, interview, offer. Date of first reply.

That is it. No scoring, no colour system, nothing that becomes a hobby. The discipline is filling it in at the moment you apply, when it costs ninety seconds, rather than reconstructing it later, which never happens.

What it tells you after twenty rows

Reply rate by source, which usually reveals that one of your channels produces nothing at all. Reply rate on tailored versus untailored applications, which settles an argument people have with themselves for months. The proportion of your applications that went to companies of unknown eligibility — often a third or more, and every one of those is an hour spent on a lottery ticket.

And the single most useful number: your silence rate. If more than about seven in ten go completely unanswered, stop applying this week and fix your CV's machine-readability first. You are not being rejected. You are not arriving.

The point is the feedback loop

Job hunting gives you almost no feedback. Nobody writes to tell you why. So if you change six things at once and something improves, you have learned nothing you can repeat.

The ledger is how you get a loop: change one thing, watch the next twenty rows, keep what moved the number. It converts an emotional process into an ordinary problem — and ordinary problems can be solved.
